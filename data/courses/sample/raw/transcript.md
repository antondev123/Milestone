# Intro to Personal Finance in South Africa

## 1. Building a budget that survives month-end

Most of us know roughly what we earn, but very few of us know where it goes. A budget is not a punishment. It is a plan for your money so that your money does not make plans for you. Start with three numbers. First, your take-home pay, the amount that actually lands in your account after tax and UIF. Second, your fixed costs: rent, transport, airtime, debit orders, black tax, school fees. These do not move much month to month. Third, everything else: groceries, data, weekends, the small things that add up.

A simple rule to start with is fifty, thirty, twenty. Fifty percent of take-home pay goes to needs, thirty to wants, and twenty to saving or paying off debt. In South Africa, where transport alone can eat fifteen to twenty percent of a salary, the exact split will look different for you. The point is not the numbers, it is that every rand has a job before the month starts.

The most common mistake is budgeting for a perfect month. Real months have a funeral, a car problem, a cousin's wedding. Put a line in your budget called "surprises" and give it something every month, even fifty rand. When the surprise comes, you have a place to take the money from that is not your rent.

Track for one month before you change anything. Use your bank app's transaction history. Do not guess. Most people find one or two categories that shock them, and that is where the first easy win is.

## 2. Interest: the thing that works for you or against you

Interest is the price of money over time. When you save, the bank pays you interest. When you borrow, you pay the bank. The same idea, pointing in opposite directions.

Simple interest is calculated only on the original amount. If you put one thousand rand in an account paying ten percent simple interest, you earn a hundred rand every year. After three years you have one thousand three hundred.

Compound interest is calculated on the original amount plus the interest already earned. Same one thousand rand at ten percent compounded yearly: after year one you have one thousand one hundred. In year two you earn ten percent of one thousand one hundred, which is one hundred and ten, so you have one thousand two hundred and ten. Year three gives you one thousand three hundred and thirty one. The gap grows every year. Over ten years the difference between simple and compound at these rates is over five hundred rand on a thousand. Over thirty years it is thousands.

This is why starting early matters more than starting big. A twenty-five year old saving five hundred rand a month will usually end up with more at sixty than a thirty-five year old saving a thousand a month, because the early money has ten extra years of compounding.

The flip side is debt. Store cards and payday loans in South Africa can charge over twenty percent a year, and interest compounds against you. Paying only the minimum on a store card means most of your payment goes to interest and the balance barely moves. When you have both savings and expensive debt, paying the debt first is usually the better return.

## 3. Your credit score and why it follows you

A credit score is a number, usually between zero and about seven hundred and fifty depending on the bureau, that summarises how reliably you have repaid money in the past. Lenders, landlords, and sometimes employers look at it. In South Africa the main bureaus are TransUnion, Experian, and Compuscan, and you are entitled to one free credit report from each of them every year.

What builds a good score: paying accounts on time, every time, even small ones like a cellphone contract. Keeping the amount you owe on credit cards well below the limit, ideally under thirty percent. Having a long history of accounts in good standing.

What damages it: missed payments, accounts handed over to debt collectors, judgments, and applying for lots of credit in a short time, because each application is recorded.

Two practical tips. First, if you have never borrowed, you may have a thin file and lenders cannot see anything. A small cellphone contract or a clothing account paid in full on time each month is a cheap way to build a history. Second, check your report once a year. Errors are common, and an account you never opened on your report is a sign of fraud you want to catch early.

## 4. Tax basics: what SARS actually takes and gives back

If you earn a salary, your employer deducts PAYE, pay as you earn, every month and sends it to SARS. This is an estimate of your annual tax, spread across twelve months. At the end of the tax year, which runs from the first of March to the end of February, you may need to file a return so SARS can check whether you paid the right amount.

You do not pay tax on every rand. There is a tax threshold, and below it you owe nothing. Above it, tax works in brackets: the first slice of income is taxed at a low rate, the next slice at a higher rate, and so on. A common misunderstanding is that earning more can push all your income into a higher bracket. It cannot. Only the rand above each bracket line is taxed at the higher rate.

Things that reduce your tax. Retirement annuity and pension contributions are deductible up to a limit, so saving for retirement also lowers your bill today. Medical aid gives you a monthly tax credit. If you had a lot of medical expenses that your scheme did not cover, some of that can be claimed.

Tax-free savings accounts let you save up to a yearly limit with no tax on the interest, dividends, or growth. There is a lifetime limit too. This is one of the best deals SARS offers and most people under thirty have never used one.

Filing is done through eFiling or the SARS app. If your employer sent your IRP5 and you have no other income, SARS often auto-assesses you and you just confirm. If you are due a refund, it usually arrives within a few days of assessment.
